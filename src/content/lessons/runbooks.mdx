quiz:
  - q: Which runbook step is written well?
    answer: 2
    options:
      - t: "Check the logs."
        why: Which logs? For what? The person reading it at 3am has to work all of that out under pressure.
      - t: "Investigate the database and fix any issues."
        why: That's a goal, not a step. It doesn't say what to look at or what "fixed" looks like.
      - t: "Run the saved Logs Insights query checkout-errors-by-type on /quickcart/orders for the last 30 minutes. If the top error_type is OperationalError, go to step 5."
        why: It names exactly what to run and where, and says what to do with the answer. Anyone on the team could follow it.
      - t: "Ask Theo — he knows how this works."
        why: A runbook exists so that nobody has to wake Theo. And one day Theo will be on holiday.
  - q: Why write down the rollback in a runbook, not just the fix?
    answer: 0
    options:
      - t: Because the fix might make things worse, and you need a known way back
        why: Every step that changes something should say how to undo it. Under pressure, working out a reversal on the spot is how small incidents become big ones.
      - t: Because AWS requires it
        why: AWS has no such rule. It's good practice, not a requirement.
      - t: Because rollbacks are always faster than fixes
        why: Often, but not always. The point is having a tested way back, whichever is faster.
      - t: It isn't needed if the steps are written carefully
        why: Careful steps still meet surprises. A way back is what makes it safe to try.
  - q: Where should the link to the runbook live, so the person on call finds it fastest?
    answer: 3
    options:
      - t: In a shared drive folder called Runbooks
        why: That works, but someone woken at 3am has to remember it exists and search for it.
      - t: In the team chat, pinned
        why: Pinned messages get buried and go out of date.
      - t: In the code's README only
        why: Keeping runbooks with the code is good, but the person needs a direct link at the moment the alarm fires.
      - t: In the alarm's description, so it arrives in the notification
        why: The alarm is the trigger. Putting the link in its description means the first thing the person reads contains the first step.
  - q: A runbook was written a year ago and never run. What should you assume?
    answer: 1
    options:
      - t: It still works — nothing important has changed
        why: A year of changes — renamed resources, new permissions, moved dashboards — breaks runbooks quietly.
      - t: Some steps are probably wrong, and the only way to find out is a timed dry run
        why: Links rot, names change and permissions move. A dry run with a clock finds the gaps before a real incident does.
      - t: It should be deleted
        why: The knowledge in it is still useful. Test it and fix it.
      - t: It only matters for new team members
        why: Everyone reads a runbook differently at 3am than at 3pm.
  - q: What does "Last tested" on a runbook tell the reader?
    answer: 2
    options:
      - t: When it was first written
        why: That's the creation date. A runbook can be old and still recently tested.
      - t: Who is allowed to use it
        why: That's the owner or the access section.
      - t: How far to trust it — a runbook tested last month is far safer than one tested two years ago
        why: The date is a warning label. An old date means "expect surprises".
      - t: When the next incident will happen

<Part id="problem">

<Story who="QuickCart, the long rollback">

A bad release broke checkout at 11pm. Everyone knew the fix was to roll back. Theo had done it twice before — but Theo was on holiday.

Noor, on call, found a chat message from months earlier describing the steps. The second step referred to a server that no longer existed. The third needed a permission her role didn't have. She woke Sam, who had the permission but had never done a rollback. Together they worked it out.

The rollback took four minutes of actual work. It took fifty minutes to get there.

</Story>

<Case>

On 28 February 2017, a large part of Amazon S3 in the us-east-1 region stopped working for several hours, taking many websites with it. AWS's public summary explained that an engineer, following an established playbook, ran a command to remove a small number of servers. One of the inputs was typed wrong, and far more servers were removed than intended.

AWS's fixes included changing the tool so it removes capacity more slowly, and refuses to go below a safe minimum. The procedure existed and was followed. What it lacked was a guard against one mistyped value.

</Case>

A runbook turns "someone who knows" into "anyone on call". This lesson is about writing one that works when it's needed, and proving that it does.

</Part>

<Part id="idea">

A **runbook** is a written procedure for one specific situation, detailed enough that anyone on the team could follow it at 3am, half awake, without asking anyone.

It is not documentation of how the system works. It's a checklist for one job: "checkout returns 5xx", "the database disk is nearly full", "roll back a release".

### The parts of a good runbook

| Part | What it says | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Trigger** | Which alarm or symptom sends you here | So you know you're in the right runbook |
| **Impact** | What customers experience while this is happening | So you know how urgent it is, and what to tell people |
| **Steps** | Numbered, exact actions: what to open, what to run, what to look for | So nobody has to think about *how* under pressure |
| **Verification** | How to know it's fixed, as a measurable test | So you don't stop too early |
| **Rollback** | How to undo each step that changes something | So a wrong step doesn't make things worse |
| **Escalation** | Who to call if it isn't working, and when | So nobody struggles alone for an hour |
| **Owner** | The team that keeps it up to date | So someone fixes it when it goes stale |
| **Last tested** | The date of the last timed dry run, and how long it took | So the reader knows how much to trust it |

### Exact steps, not good intentions

| Vague | Exact |
|---|---|
| Check the dashboard | Open the `quickcart-checkout` dashboard (link). Is the 5xx graph above 1%? |
| Look in the logs | Run the saved Logs Insights query `checkout-errors-by-type` for the last 30 minutes |
| Check the target group | **EC2 → Target Groups → quickcart-checkout-tg → Targets**. Are all targets **Healthy**? |
| Roll back | Run the `quickcart-rollback` job with `VERSION` set to the previous version, shown on the job's last successful run |
| Make sure it works | Done when the 5xx alarm is **OK** and the 5xx rate stays under 1% for 5 minutes |

Every exact step names **where**, **what**, and **what to do with the answer**. Each step that makes a change also says how to undo it.

<Key>A runbook is only as good as its last timed test.</Key>

### Test it, and time it

A runbook nobody has run is a guess, like an alarm nobody has seen fire. Test it with a **dry run**:

| Kind of test | How | Finds |
|---|---|---|
| Read-through | Someone who didn't write it reads each step aloud and says what they'd do | Vague steps, missing names, assumptions |
| Timed dry run | Follow it for real, in a staging environment, with a stopwatch | Dead links, missing permissions, slow steps |
| Game day | Break something on purpose in staging, and hand the on-call person only the alarm | Whether the whole chain works: alarm, notification, runbook, fix |

After each test: fix every gap you found, write down the time it took, and update **Last tested**. If it took 37 minutes and your target is 15, the runbook isn't finished.

### Keep them where people will find them

- **Link each runbook from its alarm.** Put the link in the alarm's description; it's included in the notification, so the first thing the on-call person reads contains the first step.
- **Keep runbooks in the repository**, as Markdown next to the code. Changes are reviewed like code, and the history shows who changed what.
- **Automate the steps that never change.** A rollback that is always the same five commands should be one pipeline job. The runbook then says "run this job", and the job can't mistype a value. [Canary from a pipeline](/lessons/canary-pipeline/) builds one.

### A complete example: checkout returns 5xx

This is QuickCart's runbook for its most important alarm. Use it as a model.

**Runbook: checkout returns 5xx**

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Trigger | Alarm `quickcart-target-5xx` or `quickcart-alb-5xx` is in ALARM |
| Impact | Some or all customers can't place orders. They see "Something went wrong" at checkout |
| Owner | Platform team |
| Target time | Service restored within 15 minutes of the alarm |
| Last tested | Last month, dry run in staging: 14 minutes |
| Escalation | If not improving after 20 minutes, call the second on-call. If customers can't pay at all, tell the support lead |

**Step 1: confirm it's real (2 minutes).** Open the `quickcart-checkout` dashboard. If the 5xx rate is above 1%, it's real: carry on. If it's zero and the alarm has already returned to OK, note it in the alarm review and stop.

**Step 2: is the load balancer happy with the servers? (1 minute).**

<ConsolePath path="EC2 > Load Balancing > Target Groups > quickcart-checkout-tg > Targets" />

| You see | Means | Go to |
|---|---|---|
| All targets **Healthy** | The servers are up; the errors come from the app | Step 3 |
| Some or all **Unhealthy**, reason `Target.Timeout` | The load balancer can't reach them: a security group or network problem | [Where to look first](/lessons/where-to-look-first/), network path |
| Some or all **Unhealthy**, other reasons | The app is failing its health check | Step 4 — something may have been deployed |

**Step 3: what are the errors? (2 minutes).**

<ConsolePath path="CloudWatch > Logs Insights" />

Choose the log group `/quickcart/orders`, set the range to **30m**, and run:

```sql title="Logs Insights query"
filter level = "ERROR"
| stats count() as errors, avg(latency_ms) as avg_ms by error_type
| sort errors desc
```

| Part | What it means |
|---|---|
| `filter level = "ERROR"` | only error lines |
| `\| stats count() as errors, avg(latency_ms) as avg_ms` | count them and average their response time |
| `by error_type` | one row per kind of error |
| `\| sort errors desc` | the most common first |

| Top `error_type` | Means | Go to |
|---|---|---|
| `OperationalError`, around 3,000 ms | The app can't connect to the database: usually its security group | Check `quickcart-db-sg` allows 3306 from `quickcart-app-sg`, then CloudTrail for who changed it |
| `ProgrammingError`, a few ms | The database answered with an error, such as a missing table: usually a migration | Step 4 |
| Anything else | A code problem | Step 4 |

**Step 4: what changed? (2 minutes).** Look at the last two runs of the deploy pipeline. Then open **CloudTrail → Event history** with **Read only** set to `false` for the last hour. If a deploy went out in the hour before the alarm, go to step 5.

**Step 5: roll back (3 minutes).** Run the `quickcart-rollback` job with `VERSION` set to the version before the latest, shown on the job's last successful run. The on-call role has permission to run it.

- **To undo this step:** run the same job with the newer version. Only do this once the cause is fixed.

**Step 6: verify (5 minutes).** It's fixed when **both** are true:

- the 5xx alarm is back to **OK**, and
- the 5xx rate on the dashboard stays under 1% for 5 minutes in a row.

Then post in the incident channel: what happened, what you did, and the times. A [postmortem](/lessons/real-incidents/) follows within two working days.

</Part>

<Part id="doit">

<Cost kind="free">you'll write a document and time yourself. Nothing is created in AWS.</Cost>

You'll write a runbook for one QuickCart alarm, then dry-run it with a stopwatch and fix what you find. About 30 minutes. You don't need anything running: a read-through dry run works on paper, and it finds most gaps.

<Steps>

<Step title="Pick one alarm">

Choose one alarm from [Alarms that actually fire](/lessons/alarms/), other than the 5xx one, which has an example above. Good choices:

| Alarm | Situation |
|---|---|
| `quickcart-unhealthy-hosts` | A server is failing its health checks |
| `quickcart-db-connections` | The database is running out of connections |
| `quickcart-db-storage` | The database disk is nearly full |

</Step>

<Step title="Write it from the template">

Create a file called `runbooks/` followed by a short name, such as `runbooks/db-storage.md`, and copy this template into it:

```text title="runbooks/db-storage.md"
# Runbook: <what is happening>

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Trigger | <the alarm name, and its condition> |
| Impact | <what customers experience> |
| Owner | <the team> |
| Target time | <restored within N minutes of the alarm> |
| Last tested | never |
| Escalation | <who to call, and after how long> |

## Step 1: confirm it's real (<N> minutes)
<exactly what to open, and what number means it's real>

## Step 2: <name> (<N> minutes)
<where to go, what to run, what to look for>
<what each possible answer means, and which step to go to>

## Step 3: fix (<N> minutes)
<the exact action>
To undo this step: <the exact way back>

## Step 4: verify (<N> minutes)
Fixed when: <a measurable test, and for how long>
```

Replace everything in angle brackets. For each step, write how many minutes you think it will take.

<See>

A runbook of four to six steps. Every step names a place, an action and what to do with the answer. Every step that changes something has a way back.

</See>

</Step>

<Step title="Read it through as a stranger">

Read each step aloud, and for each one ask:

| Question | If the answer is no |
|---|---|
| Would someone who's never seen this system know exactly where to click? | Add the console path or the link |
| Does it say what to do with each possible answer? | Add the branches |
| Could the on-call person do it with their own permissions? | Note the permission needed, and check it |
| Is "fixed" something you can measure? | Replace it with a number and a duration |

Mark every gap you find.

</Step>

<Step title="Dry-run it with a stopwatch">

Start a stopwatch on your phone. Pretend the alarm has just fired, and follow your runbook step by step. If you have the environment from [A self-healing environment](/projects/02-self-healing-environment/) or a staging copy, do the steps for real. If not, open each console page the step names and find each thing it mentions, without changing anything.

Write down how long each step really took next to your estimate.

<See>

At least one step took much longer than you expected. That's normal on a first run, and it's exactly what the dry run is for.

</See>

</Step>

<Step title="Fix the gaps and record the test">

For every step that was slow or unclear, make it exact. Then update the header:

| Field | Enter |
|---|---|
| Last tested | today's date, `dry run` or `read-through`, and the total time |

If the total is over your target time, run it again after the fixes.

</Step>

<Step title="Link it from the alarm">

When the alarm exists, put the runbook's link in its description:

<ConsolePath path="CloudWatch > Alarms > All alarms > the alarm > Actions > Edit" />

Choose **Next** until you reach **Add name and description**.

| Field | Enter |
|---|---|
| Alarm description | One line on what it means, then `Runbook:` and the link, such as `https://example.com/runbooks/db-storage` |

<See>

The link in the alarm's details. The next notification will include it.

</See>

If you deleted your alarms after the last lesson, keep the description ready for when you build them again in the projects.

</Step>

<Step title="Clean up" cleanup>

Nothing was created in AWS, so there's nothing to delete. Keep your runbook file: put it in the repository next to your QuickCart code, where you'll use it in [A self-healing environment](/projects/02-self-healing-environment/).

<See>

One tested runbook, with a real date in **Last tested**.

</See>

</Step>

</Steps>
