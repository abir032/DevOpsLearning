<Part id="problem">

<Story who="QuickCart, after the outage">

Checkout went down for 25 minutes on a Friday evening. On Monday, the founder announced a new rule: **QuickCart must never go down again.** No releases on Fridays. Then no releases without two approvals. Then no releases in the last week of the month.

Three months later, checkout is no more reliable than before. But almost nothing new has shipped, and the few releases that do go out are huge, because everything waited for them. Meanwhile Sam, on the other side of the team, argues that one outage a quarter is fine and they should ship faster. Nobody can say who's right.

</Story>

<Case title="A very common mistake: promising 100%">

Teams and managers often promise that a service will "never go down". It can't be done. Hardware fails, networks drop packets, and every release carries some risk. The only way to get close is to stop changing anything, which is a different kind of failure.

It's also wasted effort. A customer's own phone, Wi-Fi and mobile network fail far more often than a well-run service does. Past a certain point, extra reliability is invisible to users, while each extra "nine" costs a lot more than the last.

</Case>

QuickCart needs a number that says how reliable is reliable enough, and a rule for what to do when it isn't.

</Part>

<Part id="idea">

Three terms, each built on the one before.

| Term | What it is | QuickCart's example |
|---|---|---|
| **SLI**, service level indicator | Something you measure about what users experience. Usually: good events ÷ all events | Of all order requests, the share that succeeded in under one second |
| **SLO**, service level objective | The target for that SLI, over a window of time | 99.9% of order requests succeed in under one second, over 30 days |
| **Error budget** | The failure the SLO allows: 100% minus the SLO | 0.1% of order requests may fail or be slow each 30 days |

<YouKnow title="You already know this: crash-free sessions">

Your crash reporting tool shows a **crash-free sessions** rate, maybe 99.8%. That's an SLI: good sessions divided by all sessions. If your team says "we keep crash-free sessions above 99.5%", that's an SLO. The 0.5% of sessions that may crash is the error budget.

</YouKnow>

### Choosing the SLI

A good SLI measures what users feel, at the thing they care about. For each important thing users do, ask two questions: **did it work**, and **was it fast enough**?

| Counts as | Example | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Good | `201 Created` in 180 ms | It worked, quickly |
| Bad | `500` or `503`: a server error | QuickCart failed the user |
| Bad | `201` but after 3 seconds | It worked, but too slowly to feel like it did |
| Left out | `404` for an order that doesn't exist | The user asked for something wrong. QuickCart answered correctly |

CPU use, memory and server counts are useful for engineers, but they aren't SLIs. CPU can be at 90% while every customer is happy.

### What the nines mean

The number of nines sounds small. The difference isn't:

| SLO | Error budget | In a 30-day month, that's |
|---|---|---|
| 99% | 1% | 7 hours 12 minutes of failure |
| 99.5% | 0.5% | 3 hours 36 minutes |
| 99.9% | 0.1% | 43 minutes |
| 99.95% | 0.05% | 22 minutes |
| 99.99% | 0.01% | 4 minutes 19 seconds |

At 99.99%, one bad deploy that takes 20 minutes to undo uses the budget four times over. Each extra nine needs much more automation, more redundancy and more careful releases. Pick the lowest target your users would be happy with, not the highest you can say.

### Spending the budget

The error budget turns an argument into a number. Failure you can afford is budget to **spend**: on releases, on experiments, on moving fast. The team agrees an **error budget policy** in advance:

| Budget left | What the team does |
|---|---|
| More than half | Ship as normal. Try bolder changes |
| A quarter to a half | Keep shipping, but hold back risky changes and look at what used the budget |
| Less than a quarter | Only small, low-risk changes |
| None | Freeze feature releases. Work on reliability until the budget recovers |

This settles QuickCart's argument. If there's budget left, Sam is right: ship. If it's gone, the founder is right: slow down. Nobody decides by who shouts loudest.

### SLO or SLA?

An **SLA**, service level agreement, is a promise to customers with a penalty, usually money back, if you miss it. AWS publishes SLAs for its services. An SLO is your own internal target. Set your SLO tighter than any SLA you offer, so you find out you're slipping before it costs you.

<Key>An SLO says how reliable is reliable enough. The error budget is the failure you can afford, and you spend it on shipping.</Key>

</Part>

<Part id="see">

Pick an SLO, then make things go wrong. Watch how much each failure costs, and what the policy says to do. Try the same failures at 99% and at 99.99%.

<ErrorBudget />

**What to notice:** the same 20-minute outage is a small dent at 99%, half the budget at 99.9%, and four times the whole budget at 99.99%. The SLO you choose decides how much room you have to move fast.

</Part>

<Part id="doit">

<Cost kind="free">everything runs on your laptop, on a sample log file.</Cost>

You'll measure an availability SLI and a speed SLI from a web server's log, using only `grep`. Then you'll scale it up to a month and decide whether QuickCart met its SLO. About 20 minutes.

<Step title="Count the orders">

QuickCart's SLI is about placing orders, which are the `POST /orders` lines.

```sh
grep -c "POST /orders" requests.log
```

| Part | What it means |
|---|---|
| `grep` | search a file for lines containing some text |
| `-c` | print only how many lines matched, not the lines themselves |
| `"POST /orders"` | the text to look for |
| `requests.log` | the file to search |

```output
10
```

Ten order attempts: that's the **total** for the SLI.

</Step>

<Step title="Count the failed orders">

A failure is a status code in the 500s: QuickCart's fault, not the customer's.

```sh
grep -E "POST /orders 5[0-9][0-9] " requests.log
```

| Part | What it means |
|---|---|
| `grep` | search for matching lines |
| `-E` | the search text is a **pattern**, not plain text |
| `POST /orders 5[0-9][0-9] ` | `POST /orders`, a space, then a `5` followed by any two digits, then a space: a status from 500 to 599 |
| `requests.log` | the file to search |

```output
2026-09-25T12:00:05Z POST /orders 503 3002ms
2026-09-25T12:00:09Z POST /orders 500 95ms
```

Two failed orders. The `404` for `/orders/1043` isn't counted: it isn't an order attempt, and a `404` means the request asked for something that doesn't exist, which isn't a failure of QuickCart.

**Availability SLI** = good ÷ total = (10 − 2) ÷ 10 = **80%**. A terrible minute.

<Mistake title="searching for 500 only">

A common first attempt is to search for the number 500:

```sh
grep -c "500" requests.log
```

| Part | What it means |
|---|---|
| `grep -c "500"` | count lines containing the text `500` anywhere |
| `requests.log` | the file to search |

```output
1
```

**Why:** `500` is only one of the server errors. The `503` from an overloaded server is a failure too, and so is every other code from 500 to 599. Searching for plain text can also match the wrong part of a line, like a duration of `1500ms`.

**The fix:** use the pattern above. It matches any code from 500 to 599, and only in the status position, right after `POST /orders`.

</Mistake>

</Step>

<Step title="Count the slow orders">

QuickCart's SLI also says *in under one second*. A duration with four or more digits, like `1480ms`, is a second or more.

```sh
grep -c -E "POST /orders 2[0-9][0-9] [0-9]{4,}ms" requests.log
```

| Part | What it means |
|---|---|
| `grep -c -E` | count the lines matching a pattern |
| `POST /orders 2[0-9][0-9] ` | a successful order: status 200 to 299 |
| `[0-9]{4,}ms` | four or more digits, then `ms`: 1000 milliseconds or more |
| `requests.log` | the file to search |

```output
2
```

Two orders worked, but took over a second. For QuickCart's full SLI, *succeeded in under one second*, the good orders are 10 − 2 failed − 2 slow = 6. That's **60%**.

</Step>

<Step title="Scale it up to a month">

A minute of log shows how to count, not whether QuickCart met its target. Here are a full month's numbers, counted the same way:

| Count | September |
|---|---|
| Order attempts | 1,200,000 |
| Failed or slower than one second | 1,450 |
| Good | 1,198,550 |

Work out three numbers. You can use the calculator on your Mac.

| Question | Working | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| What was the SLI? | 1,198,550 ÷ 1,200,000 | 99.88% |
| How big was the error budget, at a 99.9% SLO? | 0.1% of 1,200,000 | 1,200 bad orders |
| How much of it was used? | 1,450 ÷ 1,200 | 121%: over budget |

<See>

QuickCart **missed** its 99.9% SLO in September, by 250 orders. Under the policy in [the idea](#idea), feature releases pause until the budget recovers.

</See>

</Step>

<Cheat>

| Term or command | In one line |
|---|---|
| SLI | What you measure: good events ÷ all events |
| SLO | The target for the SLI over a window, like 99.9% over 30 days |
| Error budget | 100% minus the SLO. The failure you can afford |
| SLA | A promise to customers with a penalty. Looser than your SLO |
| 99.9% | About 43 minutes of failure a month |
| 99.99% | About 4 minutes a month |
| Good vs bad | 5xx and too slow are bad. 4xx usually aren't |
| Error budget policy | Agreed in advance: what happens as the budget runs out |
| `grep -c "TEXT" FILE` | Count matching lines |
| `grep -E "PATTERN" FILE` | Match a pattern, like `5[0-9][0-9]` |

</Cheat>
